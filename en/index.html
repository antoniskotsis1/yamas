History

From the ancient spit
to pita on the go

Souvlaki and gyros are the taste of the Greek street today, but their story starts thousands of years ago. A short guide: where they come from, how they differ and how to eat them like a Greek.

  1. Bronze Age

    Mycenaeans roast meat on skewers over portable grills. Archaeologists have found clay skewer rests more than 3,000 years old.

  2. 8th c. BC

    In Homer’s Iliad, warriors thread meat onto obeloi — iron spits. The obol coin takes its name from them.

  3. 1922

    Refugees from Asia Minor bring the vertical rotating spit to Greece — the ancestor of today’s gyros.

  4. 1960s–70s

    Gyros in pita with fries and tzatziki becomes a street classic in Athens and Thessaloniki.

  5. Today

    Souvlaki and gyros are ambassadors of Greek food around the world — and now on Świętokrzyska Street too.

Souvlaki

from the Greek souvla — spit

What it is
Chunks of marinated meat — usually pork or chicken — threaded onto a skewer and grilled. Say “soo-VLAH-kee”.
How to eat it
Straight off the stick with a squeeze of lemon and a slice of bread — or wrapped in pita with tomato, onion and tzatziki. In Athens the skewer is called kalamaki.

Gyros

from the Greek gyros — turn

What it is
Layers of seasoned meat roasted on a vertical, rotating spit and shaved off in thin slices. Say “YEE-ros”.
How to eat it
Classically in a pita wrapped in paper: peel the paper back as you go and eat from the top. In Greek you order it ap’ óla — with everything. Prefer cutlery? Get a merída, a plated portion.

Pita & tzatziki

the essential duo

What it is
Greek pita is thick, soft and pocketless — warmed on the griddle with a little olive oil so it rolls without tearing. Tzatziki is thick yoghurt with cucumber, garlic and olive oil.
How to eat it
Tzatziki goes with everything: gyros, souvlaki, fries or just pita. And fries inside the pita? In Greece that’s standard, not a gimmick.

  • Τυλιχτό

  • Μερίδα
    merída

    a plated portion

  • Καλαμάκι
    kalamáki

    meat on a skewer

  • Γεια μας!
    yamas

    cheers! (literally “our health”)
